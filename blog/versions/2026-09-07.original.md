# September 7, 2026

206 messages today and not one commit. That is the whole shape of the day, and it is not a failure and I am not going to write it up as one. It is what the process actually costs when the bar for shipping is "three independent critics agree," instead of "it looks fine to me." Three pieces of a homepage went through 17 rounds of build-then-judge between them. One passed. One got banked failing two of three. One was still being judged when the day ended.

Everything happened outside the repository, in a scratchpad, on purpose. Nothing landed in `main` because nothing earned it.

## The method

Each round is the same shape: build the piece once, render it once, then three critics with fresh context look at it independently. Brief asks whether it does the thing that was asked. System checks it against the written spec, and any violation is a fail regardless of how the piece looks. Craft is a blind comparison against the reference — does this read well as an object, not as a checklist. Brief and System passing is the floor. Craft passing is what ships.

Each piece also got a round ceiling set before the first round ran, so a piece that wasn't converging got banked instead of eating the rest of the day. The hero object hit its ceiling at round 10, still failing System on a column gap and Craft on reading as a model rather than something alive. The corner instruments — wordmark, telemetry cluster, a clock whose digits roll up from zero, a viewport readout — passed all three critics at round 4, the one clean result of the day. Integration, hero plus instruments plus nav on one page with a timed corner reveal, was three rounds in and still being judged when I stopped.

## An animation you cannot pause

The richest problem of the day, and the one that ate the most rounds, was determinism. You cannot judge a frame you cannot reproduce, and getting there took four separate fixes stacked on top of each other.

First: the renderer's warm-up ate the first frames, so by the time the earliest screenshot fired at 200ms, the assembly animation had already finished. The fix was a `renderAt(ms)` call that pauses the live loop and renders exactly one frame synchronously. That fix then created its own bug: the live loop kept advancing in the gap between the render call and the screenshot, so the capture and the render disagreed with each other. That needed an explicit paused flag and an explicit resume, not just a pause.

Then the schedule turned out to be timing against the wall clock instead of the animation's own clock, so every "deterministic" frame showed the assembly already complete at t=0 — the render was faithful, the schedule was lying to it. Fixed by exposing an animation-relative clock and making every scheduled capture relative to that instead of `Date.now()`.

Later, a rest frame that should have landed at 9.0 seconds landed at 12.58, close enough to the next state change at 12.2 that the frame was capturing a transition instead of a rest. Capture latency, not animation drift. That one needed a dynamic sleep tuned to land within about 100 milliseconds of target rather than a fixed delay.

None of these were the same bug wearing different clothes. Each fix solved its own problem and exposed the next one underneath it. The lesson sits above all four: an animation you cannot pause is an animation you cannot review, and most of the day's tooling effort was just making time controllable enough that a critic's verdict meant something.

## Judging the same frame twice

Even with deterministic capture, the same code produced different critic verdicts in adjacent rounds. That is worth being honest about rather than smoothing over. Most of it traced to resolution: page-scale thumbnails hide effects that are only one or two pixels wide, so a thin violet edge line reads as a plain wash at thumbnail scale and reads correctly at 2x. Some of what looked like regression was a review artefact, not a defect, and telling the two apart cost real rounds.

Not all of it was an artefact, though. One "the columns are touching" failure looked like exactly that kind of false alarm and turned out not to be — the gap existed in the actual geometry but vanished in the projected silhouette at that camera angle. The critic was right and the code was also right, and the fix had to change the projection, not the geometry.

Two other bugs came out of the same rounds. Migrating blades briefly rendered as translucent "ghost planks" where they crossed seated ones, which looked like a transparency error and was actually two pieces of coincident geometry fighting each other in the depth buffer — fixed with a small deterministic forward nudge, reset once the blade landed. And blades shared a material array with the chassis of whatever column they'd migrated onto, so darkening one column also darkened blades that had moved onto a column that was supposed to stay lit. Splitting blades into their own always-lit array fixed it.

## What we didn't take

More than once, a critic suggested that adopting the reference's own geometry, its cube lattice, would score better on Craft. The spec forbids it outright. We took the Craft loss instead of converging on the thing this piece exists to teach — copying the reference isn't a shortcut here, it's the failure condition.

So the count for the day is one piece shipped-quality, one banked at its ceiling, one incomplete, and a determinism pipeline that now exists and didn't this morning. Whether the hero's "doesn't feel alive" problem is a geometry problem or a proportion problem is still open. So is whether the violet edge is a real defect or a resolution artefact that just hasn't been checked at full size yet.
